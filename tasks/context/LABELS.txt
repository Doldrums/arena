# Task labels

Every task carries a `labels` block (in `suite/<id>/problem.json`, `suite/<id>/task.json`, `suite/index.json` and `catalog/tasks.json`) that says what an improved score would mean, what the candidate is, what the score is computed from and what running the task costs. The labels are assigned by rules in `scripts/task_labels.py` (family of the evaluator, unsupported category and source, and a few per-task overrides) and are regenerated by `scripts/build_suite.py`; this page is generated by `python3 scripts/task_labels.py --doc`.

Filter with the CLI (`python3 run.py list --label scientific_contribution=open_problem`), in the dashboard (the "Contribution" filter) or in `catalog/index.html`.

## 1. Scientific contribution

The main label answers one question: **if a search system improves the score, what has it produced?**

| `scientific_contribution` | tier | Criteria | Examples |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `open_problem` | 1 | The task is a stated mathematical problem (a packing, an extremal set, a tensor decomposition, an inequality constant) whose best-known value is the state of knowledge; the evaluator checks a certificate exactly; the value is comparable with the literature or a public record. A better score is a new construction or bound. | AlphaEvolve's 55 matrix-multiplication tensors, circle and hexagon packings, Heilbronn problems, kissing number in dimension 11, autocorrelation inequalities, Erdős minimum overlap, FunSearch cap sets and admissible tuples, the 57 SCiEL construction problems, the Kaggle Santa 2025 tree packing |
| `discovery` | 1 | The candidate is a model, law or predictor of **real measurements** (experimental, observational or logged data) and a better fit is a candidate empirical result requiring independent scientific validation. | material stress-strain equations (LLM-SR), SLDBench scaling laws fitted to real training runs, The Station's RNA, single-cell and neural-activity tasks, LaSR's BIG-Bench scaling law |
| `rediscovery` | 2 | Equation recovery on data generated from a known or synthetic model. Scientific in form, but the answer is known: the score measures a discovery method, not new knowledge. | LLM-SR oscillators and E. coli growth, LLM-SRBench transform/synth subsets, Feynman and KAN-style targets |
| `method` | 2 | An algorithm, heuristic or optimiser scored on benchmark instances of a computational problem. Improvements are research contributions in operations research, numerics, optimisation or IR when they beat known methods, but do not settle a scientific question. | TSP/CVRP/bin-packing/scheduling heuristics (EoH, ReEvo, MCTS-AHD, A2DEPT, DGA2D), ODE integrators and root finders, BBOB and Bayesian optimisers (LLaMEA, FunBO), ALE-Bench contests, RL update rules, retrieval scoring functions, SAT solvers |
| `engineering` | 3 | A kernel, system configuration, reward function, policy, ML pipeline or program whose value is practical performance in a specific stack. | ADRS systems tasks, the Anthropic kernel simulator, CUDA/Trainium/MLX kernels, Eureka-style rewards, MLE-bench pipelines, compiler heuristics, AlgoTune speed-ups |
| `benchmark` | 3 | A prompt, agent scaffold or workflow scored by running a language model on a benchmark. The score measures model behaviour under the candidate, so improvements are about the model, not a scientific object. | prompt optimisation on BBH/GSM8K/Instruction Induction, ADAS/AFlow agents, SWE-bench and terminal-bench agents, ARC solvers, creative writing judged by a model |

**Important to science** means the task addresses a substantive scientific or real-world problem, and a cited research paper's **main objective** is solving that problem or advancing understanding of it. The evidence must explain the underlying problem, why it matters (for example, health, the environment, practical systems, or scientific understanding), and how the local task represents it.

A task's use to evaluate an agent, framework, or general-purpose model does **not** establish this importance. A benchmark, dataset, tool, scientific topic, or a paper merely mentioning the problem is also insufficient. A domain-specific AI method can qualify when solving the underlying scientific problem is the paper's main objective. Mathematical and computational problems can qualify through their contribution to understanding or practical capabilities; a generic synthetic test function does not qualify merely because it has a paper citation.

Each supported task has `labels.important_to_science = true`, `science_relevance.criterion = primary_research_goal`, an explicit `scientific_problem` and `importance_rationale`, and paper records containing the title, link, `primary_goal`, and `task_relation`. Curated decisions live in `scripts/science_evidence_overrides.json`. Original agent-paper task locators remain provenance but confer no automatic importance label. Local simplifications and differences from the paper's experiments are stated in the evidence.

Importance is independent of contribution type, tier, implementation origin, and readiness. A better local score need not be a discovery or a published record.

`science_relevance.status = unreviewed` and `important_to_science = false` mean supporting evidence has not yet been established here, **not** that a task is unimportant. Statistics show supported versus evidence-unreviewed tasks, with all canonical tasks in the denominator. The legacy `science_tier` describes contribution type only and must not be used as an importance ranking.

Decision rules, in order:

1. Is the objective a mathematical quantity of a checkable object, and is a better value a better bound or construction for a problem people state in the literature (or a public open competition on such an object)? Then `open_problem`.
2. Otherwise, is the candidate fitted to real measurements? Then `discovery`. Fitted to data generated from a known law? Then `rediscovery`.
3. Otherwise, is the candidate a general algorithm judged on instances of a well-defined computational problem (routing, scheduling, SAT, numerical integration, black-box optimisation, ranking, RL)? Then `method`.
4. Otherwise, does the score come from running a language model on benchmark items (prompt, scaffold, agent, workflow, judged text)? Then `benchmark`.
5. Everything else that is judged by measured performance of a system (latency, throughput, task success in a simulator, competition metric of an ML pipeline) is `engineering`.

Rules 1 and 2 are applied at task level (a paper can have tasks in several classes); a documented-only task (`unsupported` protocol) is labelled by what the paper evaluates, so the labels describe the problem, not whether it runs here.

## 2. Other axes

| Axis | Values | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `artifact` | construction, program, equation, optimizer, prompt, agent, reward, policy, kernel, configuration, model, environment | what the candidate is |
| `area` | mathematics, optimization, scientific_computing, machine_learning, language_and_agents, software_engineering, systems_and_hardware, robotics_and_control, biology, physics, chemistry, materials_science, information_retrieval, finance, games, theoretical_computer_science, cosmology_and_astrophysics, particle_physics, quantum_information, climate_dynamics, earth_and_environment | the field the problem targets (the coarse "what kind of problem is this" axis; natural-science tasks are split by discipline from the task's dataset) |
| `domain` | mathematics, combinatorial_optimisation, continuous_optimisation, numerical_computing, machine_learning, systems, robotics, natural_science, language, software_engineering, games, information_retrieval, finance | the finer field used by the assignment rules |
| `data` | none, synthetic, public_benchmark, real_measurements, simulator, undistributed, proprietary | what the score is computed from (`none`: a mathematical object, no data) |
| `verification` | exact, simulation, sampled, external, none | how the evaluator establishes the score: exact objective of a checkable object; deterministic run of a program, judge or simulator; statistical estimate on a sample (usually with a model in the loop); an outside judge or service; documented only |
| `reference` | published_value, leaderboard, none | whether an exact published value (AlphaEvolve notebook constructions, matrix-multiplication ranks) or a public competition record is embedded and comparable |
| `resources` | cpu, model_api, gpu, docker, simulator, network, account, unavailable | derived from the requirements |
| `cost` | fast (< 10 s), medium (10 to 120 s), slow (> 120 s), not_run | measured seed-evaluation time in the last execution test |

## 5. Using the labels

- **Choosing science tasks.** Filter `important_to_science=true` and inspect the underlying problem, why it matters, each linked paper's main research objective, and the task connection. Then choose contribution types and implementation requirements appropriate to the experiment. Contribution tiers do not rank scientific importance; reaching a baseline or fitting real data does not by itself establish a new scientific result.
- **Cost planning.** `resources` and `cost` say what a task needs and how long one evaluation of the seed took; `model_api` tasks also cost money per evaluation (see `docs/RESULTS.md`).
- **Extending the rules.** Add a family to `FAMILY`, an unsupported group to `UNSUPPORTED` or a task id to `OVERRIDES` in `scripts/task_labels.py`, add a rationale line for a new source, then run `python3 scripts/build_suite.py --sciel ../SCiEL` and `python3 scripts/task_labels.py --doc`. The tests check that every task has labels from the vocabulary above.
